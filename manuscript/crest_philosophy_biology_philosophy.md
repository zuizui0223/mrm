# What Counts as the Same Ecological State?
## A Contract-Relative Account of State-Representation Adequacy

## Abstract

Ecology routinely compresses heterogeneous configurations into shared states used for prediction, comparison, and management. Existing work already provides mature accounts of ecosystem identity, ecological model adequacy, intervention-sensitive states, predictive state abstraction, partial observability, causal abstraction, and purpose-sensitive scientific representation. We ask a narrower question: when several ecological configurations are assigned the same coarse state, what scientific commitments are being made by that equivalence? We distinguish four adequacy questions in the current program: whether the equivalence remains sufficient under declared future operations, preserves inherited meaning after structural change, supports prediction across retained mechanism alternatives, and is licensed by the available evidence. These questions are anchored respectively by an open-future interface lower bound, a unique coarsest source-relative repair, mechanism-safe deterministic/typed/set-valued reporting, and an evidence-relative reportability criterion with a required target-safe refinement. The same coarse state can therefore fail for different reasons, and the appropriate response differs: retain additional predictive information, repair inherited semantics, preserve or resolve mechanism ambiguity, or withhold a distinction that the evidence has not earned. Contract-relative adequacy is not unrestricted relativism: once a scientific contract is declared, proposed state merges can be tested and can fail for explicit formal reasons. We present CREST as a theorem-grounded synthesis for making ecological state-equivalence commitments explicit; no claim is made that these four audits are exhaustive, commuting, jointly minimal, or the first general theory of representational adequacy.

**Keywords:** philosophy of ecology; ecological state; scientific representation; model adequacy; causal abstraction; uncertainty

## 1. From ecosystem identity to state-representation adequacy

Ecologists have long had to decide what counts as a system, a state, a regime, or a functionally equivalent configuration. Philosophy of ecology has examined the identity and continuity of ecological systems, including dynamical accounts of ecosystem identity and distinctions among different senses of ecological identity (Cumming & Collier, 2005; Collier & Cumming, 2011; Delettre, 2021). Ecological modelling has likewise developed explicit adequacy protocols (Getz et al., 2018), intervention-sensitive State-and-Transition Models (Stringham et al., 2003), adaptive-management and POMDP approaches to hidden state and model uncertainty, and methods for task-relevant state aggregation (Nicol & Chadès, 2012; Fackler & Pacifici, 2014), alongside extensive work on ecological model transferability (Yates et al., 2018). In adjacent formal fields, causal states and causal abstraction already provide mature accounts of prediction- or intervention-preserving coarse representations (Shalizi & Crutchfield, 2001; Beckers & Halpern, 2019). CREST begins from this literature rather than from the claim that ecological representation was previously treated as context-free.

A narrower commitment is nevertheless easy to leave implicit. Let a coarse state representation assign ecological configurations \(x\in X\) to labels \(q(x)\in Q\). Whenever \(q(x)=q(y)\), the representation treats at least some differences between \(x\) and \(y\) as irrelevant to the work assigned to that label. The important question is not simply whether the simplification is useful. It is **which differences the state variable commits us to ignoring for the scientific work assigned to it**. A state label used to summarize the present, forecast future interventions, transfer an inherited ecological classification, average over mechanism uncertainty, and support an empirical report is being asked to satisfy more than one kind of adequacy.

This question is narrower than the identity of the ecological system itself. A lake may remain numerically the same ecological system through an intervention while a particular management-state variable ceases to be an adequate predictive description. Conversely, two different systems may be assigned the same coarse functional state for a particular comparison. The philosophical issue addressed here is therefore not whether ecological systems possess one true identity criterion. It is the scientific warrant for a proposed equivalence relation among configurations used as a state representation. This distinction allows CREST to build on ecological-identity theory without turning state-representation adequacy into a rival metaphysics of ecosystems.

CREST separates four adequacy questions in the present theorem program. **Future sufficiency** asks whether an enlarged legal future can expose a distinction that the coarse state erased. **Semantic coherence** asks whether an inherited state meaning remains operationally valid after structural replacement. **Mechanism robustness** asks whether retained response mechanisms agree on the future prediction requested from the state. **Evidential licensing** asks whether the available experiment and observation contract actually justify reporting the distinction that the prediction requires. These are not asserted to be an exhaustive taxonomy of ecological adequacy, nor are they assumed to be generally independent. They are kept separate because the current formalizations begin from different objects, quantify over different alternatives, and return different failure certificates and remedies.

The resulting position is contract-relative without being arbitrary. Scientific representation and model evaluation are already widely understood as purpose-sensitive rather than reducible to one context-free measure of accuracy or fidelity (Giere, 2010; Parker, 2020; Bokulich & Parker, 2021), and perspectival accounts need not abandon realism (Massimi, 2022). CREST adds no priority claim here. Scientists choose which futures, inherited meanings, candidate mechanisms, observations, and targets belong to a model contract; CREST does not prove that one such contract is uniquely correct for nature. But once a contract is declared, the proposed state equivalence is no longer protected by convenience alone. A legal future can distinguish two merged configurations; a structural replacement can make an inherited label operationally incoherent; retained mechanisms can disagree on a successor; and an evidence class can contain multiple target values. Our philosophical claim is therefore modest: **ecological state equivalence should be treated as an explicit scientific commitment rather than an invisible modelling default.**

## 2. One state label, four different tests

Consider the coarse ecological state **pollination maintained**. Suppose two configurations currently receive this same label because both sustain adequate pollination under the present community and management repertoire. CREST does not ask whether that label is simply true or false. It asks what follows when the same label is required to perform different scientific tasks.

### 2.1 Future sufficiency

Two configurations can be equivalent under every supplied closed future yet require different state information once future colonization, reconnection, dispersal, or intervention possibilities are jointly opened. CCOC formalizes this as a cross-grammar compression problem. A state representation is exact relative to a declared future grammar when configurations merged by that representation remain indistinguishable under every legal future word in the grammar. The central lower-bound family shows that each closed future can admit a small exact interface even though a jointly open future grammar requires an interface whose memory grows with the number of independently addressable dormant distinctions.

The relevant philosophical point is not that future-oriented equivalence is new. Predictive states, bisimulation, and state-abstraction traditions already define states through future or action-conditioned behavior. The distinctive use of the formal result here is comparative: the same physical family can support small closed-context descriptions and a much larger open-future description depending on which future operations the state is required to survive. What counts as safely ignorable information therefore changes with the declared future contract. The ecological lesson is not that open systems lack macrolaws, but that **present or closed-context functional equivalence need not be sufficient for an enlarged future repertoire**.

For the pollination example, two communities may be equivalent while the current management repertoire exposes no difference between them. A later connection to a substitute pollinator, a dispersal corridor, or a new intervention can make one previously dormant distinction predictive. The state label has not become retrospectively false; rather, the scientific demand placed on it has changed. Future-sufficiency failure therefore calls for retaining more predictive information or accepting an explicit lower bound on how far the system can be compressed under the enlarged repertoire.

### 2.2 Semantic coherence

A different problem appears when a state label is inherited across structural change. After pollinator turnover, the label `pollination maintained` may still be syntactically available while no longer grouping target configurations with the same legal actions or future responses. MLTR fixes the inherited source classification rather than replacing it with an unconstrained target abstraction. The carried target partition is tested for exactness relative to the declared target dynamics. When it fails, iterative refinement yields the unique coarsest exact **source-relative** repair: it introduces only those splits forced by target output, legal-action, or successor differences while preserving inherited merges that remain valid.

This is not merely another request for a better target abstraction. If the target were free to choose any partition whatsoever, an entirely different coarse graining could be smaller or more convenient. MLTR instead treats inherited meaning as a constraint on admissible repair. The scientific question is whether the old variable can continue to mean enough of what it meant before the structural change to remain a valid interface for the target system. This is why the formal output is a repair relative to one carried source law rather than a generic target optimum.

Historical context enters at the same semantic level. If different declared histories carry incompatible inherited terminal meanings that the analysis requires us to preserve, one route-free inherited label map is insufficient; the formal history result retains the minimum finite context needed to keep those carried meanings distinct. This does not imply that different histories must always produce different final unlabeled partitions. The claim is narrower: **semantic reuse imposes a constraint that ordinary target-only re-abstraction can ignore**. That problem is distinct from ecological model transferability in general, whose limitations under novel conditions are already well established (Yates et al., 2018).

In the pollination example, a state once adequate under one pollinator assemblage may need to split after turnover into configurations with and without substitute-response capacity. The remedy is not simply “remember more” in the CCOC sense. It is “repair an inherited classification while preserving as much of its old operational meaning as the target permits.” The difference matters because a management category can fail semantically even if the target system admits some other compact predictive abstraction.

### 2.3 Mechanism robustness

The same visible pollination state can also be compatible with several retained response mechanisms. Suppose the currently observed macrostate is identical under two mechanisms, but competitor removal or habitat restoration produces different successors. A single deterministic forecast is then not uniformly supported over the retained family. MRM formalizes this by collapsing raw candidates into response types: candidates that induce the same declared transition behavior are predictively equivalent, while response types that differ under some relevant action cannot automatically be forgotten.

This mechanism-relative problem should not be confused with the demand to retain full mechanism identity. MRM's minimal candidate-safe quotient explicitly allows mechanism distinctions to merge when they are irrelevant to the declared future behavior. If disagreement remains, the honest output need not be a forced choice between mechanisms. Typed or set-valued reporting can represent exactly what follows from the retained family, and active discrimination becomes a separate conditional question about whether declared interventions can resolve the remaining response ambiguity.

The philosophical point is therefore not that mechanisms are uncertain or that model pluralism is new. Adaptive-management and POMDP traditions already combine structural/model uncertainty with intervention and observation (Fackler & Pacifici, 2014). The point is about the adequacy of the *state equivalence*: **mechanism ambiguity is a predictive-state issue only where retained alternatives make different predictions under the declared action and target contract**. A state representation can be future-sufficient relative to each candidate model considered separately while still fail to license one candidate-independent deterministic prediction over the retained family.

This failure calls for a different remedy from future insufficiency or semantic non-portability. One may retain the relevant response-type distinction, report a set of possible successors, or perform a discriminating intervention. Treating the situation simply as “insufficient state detail” would hide the fact that the missing distinction is indexed by unresolved mechanism alternatives rather than by a larger future grammar or inherited semantics.

### 2.4 Evidential licensing

Finally, a distinction can be prediction-relevant without being one that current observations license us to report. Camera records, visitation observations, eDNA, or experiments may leave multiple target-relevant worlds compatible. CED begins from the record induced by a declared finite experiment and observation contract. A deterministic target report is licensed exactly when the target is constant on the compatible evidence class. If compatible worlds imply different target values, the sharp honest report remains ambiguity-explicit.

CED also constructs a target-safe quotient, but its interpretation is crucial. The quotient describes the minimum additional resolution sufficient for target-constant, action-stable deterministic tracking while preserving the initial record partition. It is **not** a state that the current data have already identified. If one compatible record class spans several target-safe blocks, then the target-safe refinement states what the evidence would need to resolve, not what it has already earned. Observation architecture further constrains whether a nominal distinction can be credited: repeated observations within one shared failure domain do not automatically provide the same guarantee as independent failure diversity.

This evidential distinction is adjacent to adequacy-for-purpose and partial-observability frameworks (Parker, 2020; Nicol & Chadès, 2012), not a replacement for them. CREST uses the CED results to enforce a philosophical separation between **what the scientific state representation would need to distinguish** and **what the available evidence justifies the researcher in reporting**. Confusing these levels would turn a representational requirement into an empirical assertion.

In the pollination example, a model may correctly identify substitute-response capacity as the distinction needed for a management prediction while the field observations remain compatible with both possibilities. The appropriate remedy is not to refine the reported state by fiat. It is to preserve ambiguity, strengthen the evidence contract, or choose a risk-limited experiment from the declared policy family. This is an epistemic failure of licensing, not necessarily a failure of the predictive or semantic state structure itself.

## 3. Why four failures are not one adequacy objective

The most important philosophical claim of CREST is not that there are four boxes rather than three or five. Nor is it that a sufficiently rich mathematical model could not encode all of the relevant variables at once. A POMDP, causal model, or other decision framework may contain actions, histories, alternative mechanisms, observation models, and target variables in one formal object. The reason to distinguish the four audits is that **encoding the ingredients together does not make the scientific obligations synonymous**.

One way to see this is to compare the starting points of the four problems. Future sufficiency begins with a proposed coarse representation and asks what happens when the admissible future grammar is widened. The relevant counterfactual varies over future operations. Semantic coherence instead begins with one accepted source representation and constrains every admissible target repair to respect the meaning carried from that source. The central variation is over target states and structural histories under an inherited semantic constraint. Mechanism robustness holds the observed macrostate space fixed and varies over retained response laws. Evidential licensing holds a target/reporting question fixed and varies over worlds compatible with a record and reliability contract. These are not merely different variables inside the same equation; they determine what counts as a legitimate comparison in the first place.

The quantifier order matters for the scientific interpretation. In CCOC, each closed context may optimize its own exact interface before those minima are compared with the interface required by a jointly open grammar. In MLTR, by contrast, one source partition is fixed first and target refinement is allowed only relative to the labels inherited from it. Reversing that order destroys the notion of source-relative repair. In MRM, the issue is not to choose the best mechanism and then compress its states; it is to ask what can be reported uniformly while several response types remain retained. In CED, the issue is not to define the target-safe distinctions and then assume they have been observed; the evidence class comes first, and deterministic reporting is permitted only when the evidence actually supports the target claim.

These differences also explain why one scalar `adequacy score` would be philosophically misleading. A single score could be useful for optimization, but it would erase the kind of claim that failed. Consider a state variable that is perfectly future-sufficient under a declared grammar and semantically coherent after structural transfer. It may nevertheless be mechanism-non-robust because two retained response laws disagree. Collecting more state variables from the same current system may not solve that disagreement; the relevant remedy may be a discriminating intervention or an ambiguity-explicit prediction. Conversely, a mechanism-safe state could be mathematically well defined while the available observation system cannot determine which state obtains. The remedy is then evidential rather than representational. A low aggregate adequacy score would not tell us which problem we have.

The distinction is clearest when requirements and achievements are separated. CCOC can identify distinctions that an open-future interface must retain. MLTR can identify distinctions required to repair inherited semantics. MRM can identify response distinctions that must remain if deterministic prediction is to hold uniformly over a retained mechanism family. CED can then tell us that the current record still does not license one of those distinctions. The mathematical structures may all involve partitions or refinements, but the scientific direction of fit differs. Some partitions specify what the representation must remember; some constrain what counts as preserving an inherited meaning; some represent unresolved predictive alternatives; some characterize what evidence can warrant.

This is why the remedy contrast is not a presentational convenience. Future insufficiency calls for additional interface information or a recognition that no comparably small open interface is available under the declared grammar. Semantic non-portability calls for source-relative repair, potentially with explicit history context. Mechanism non-robustness calls for typed or set-valued prediction, retention of response-relevant mechanism distinctions, or discrimination. Evidential non-resolvability calls for ambiguity-retaining reporting or stronger evidence. These remedies are not interchangeable. More observations do not by themselves repair inherited semantics; a source-relative semantic split does not resolve mechanism uncertainty; choosing a candidate mechanism does not license an observation-dependent claim; and an evidence-rich description can still be an inadequate open-future abstraction.

A second objection is that the distinctions are merely bookkeeping because a large enough state variable could encode everything. But maximal encoding is not the scientific problem. Ecological state variables are useful precisely because they suppress detail. The problem is to understand which suppressions are licensed for which uses. A universal microscopic state could trivially preserve future behavior, historical provenance, mechanism identity, and evidence records, yet it would evade rather than solve the representational problem. Model adequacy, causal abstraction, and state aggregation are concerned with principled loss of detail. CREST's diagnostic claim is that the *reason a loss is unacceptable* matters to how the representation should be revised or how the result should be reported.

This point also clarifies the relation to Getz et al.'s (2018) broader model-adequacy programme and Parker's (2020) adequacy-for-purpose account. CREST does not replace either. It takes one particular object that such frameworks must eventually assess—a coarse state equivalence—and asks how several different adequacy obligations bear on that object. The four audits can therefore be understood as a decomposition of a representational commitment inside a broader adequacy-for-purpose philosophy. Their value lies less in adding another global criterion than in preventing one kind of adequacy from being mistaken for another.

Nor does the argument require the four audits to be logically independent in every possible formal model. In some applications one failure may imply or strongly correlate with another. A poor observation system may make mechanism discrimination impossible; a structural replacement may also enlarge the action grammar; a new experiment may reveal the same distinction that an open-future argument says must be retained. CREST's present claim is weaker: the *questions* and *remedies* are distinct enough that passing one should not silently be reported as satisfying the others. Whether particular coupled systems make the audits commute, imply one another, or share a joint minimum is an open mathematical question rather than a premise of the philosophy.

The result is a failure-oriented view of adequacy. Instead of asking only whether a state representation is adequate, we ask: **adequate with respect to which obligation, and what kind of failure would show that it is not?** For ecological practice this can change both diagnosis and communication. A manager told that a state category is “inadequate” needs to know whether the problem is unmodelled future access, loss of inherited semantic validity, unresolved mechanism alternatives, or insufficient evidence. The word `state` may look the same in each case, but the scientific correction is not.

## 4. Contract-relative does not mean arbitrary

The most natural philosophical objection is that a contract-relative account makes state identity merely conventional: choose a different purpose and obtain a different state. That conclusion does not follow. CREST concerns the adequacy of a **scientific representation**, not the claim that underlying ecological processes depend on our descriptions. The contracts specify which consequences a representation is expected to preserve. Given those commitments, failure can be objective relative to the model: two merged configurations produce different legal future traces; a carried label fails to factor target dynamics; two retained response types disagree; or one evidence class supports incompatible target values.

This allows CREST to remain compatible with realist approaches to ecological systems while also taking perspectival and pragmatic representation seriously (Giere, 2010; Massimi, 2022). A coarse state need not mirror a unique intrinsic partition of nature to be scientifically constrained. Conversely, the fact that a state variable is useful in one context does not authorize its unqualified reuse in another. The philosophical burden shifts from asking whether a coarse state is simply “real” to asking **what invariances and reporting rights its use commits us to**.

The word `contract` is intended to make this burden explicit, not to suggest legalism or unrestricted choice. A future grammar specifies which operations count as relevant counterfactual tests. An inherited-semantic contract specifies which earlier meanings a target representation is required to preserve. A mechanism contract specifies which response alternatives remain live. An evidence contract specifies what observation, detection, and risk assumptions license a report. These choices can be debated, revised, or empirically challenged. But once they are stated, the adequacy question becomes inspectable rather than implicit.

This distinction is especially important in ecology because boundaries, scales, and interventions are scientifically consequential rather than purely descriptive choices. A plot, island, watershed, or community can be represented at many levels. CREST does not tell ecologists which level is uniquely correct. It asks what follows once a state label at a chosen level is asked to support a particular future, transfer, mechanism, and reporting task. Contract-relativity therefore belongs to a realist-compatible philosophy of scientific representation: the world constrains which merges succeed, even though the scientific purpose helps determine which constraints are relevant.

## 5. Consequences for ecological explanation and measurement

The four-contract view changes how state variables should be discussed even before one attempts a joint formal model. First, ecological explanations should distinguish *descriptive sameness* from *predictive sameness*. Two communities may share present observables while differ in future responses. A state label adequate for reporting current function should not be assumed to support interventions that were not part of the original contract.

Second, transfer of ecological categories should be treated as a semantic operation rather than a mere relabelling. Functional groups, resilience classes, occupancy categories, or management states can be reused across sites or time periods only insofar as their operational meaning survives the structural change relevant to the prediction. MLTR's source-relative repair provides one exact benchmark for this idea: when the inherited classification fails, the repair should preserve old merges that remain valid rather than silently replace the old variable with a new target-only abstraction.

Third, model or mechanism uncertainty should be reflected at the level at which it changes conclusions. If alternative mechanisms produce the same relevant future, retaining their full identities in the reported state can be unnecessary. If they disagree, collapsing them into one deterministic forecast can be misleading. This suggests a reporting norm: mechanism uncertainty should be made explicit **when it changes the requested target response**, not merely because multiple mechanistic stories exist.

Fourth, measurement should be separated from representational requirement. A model can identify which distinction would be needed for a deterministic target prediction without the field protocol having resolved that distinction. This is the CED lesson. More detailed state variables do not become evidence simply because the model would benefit from them. Measurement design must earn the distinctions that downstream representation requires, and reports should remain set-valued or ambiguity-explicit when that evidence is absent.

These consequences do not amount to a universal workflow in which future sufficiency must always be checked first, followed by semantics, mechanism, and evidence. The order used in this paper is explanatory. In a field application the evidence contract may be specified first; a mechanism family may motivate the intervention set; a structural transfer problem may define the relevant target before an open-future analysis is formulated. CREST's contribution is to keep the obligations visible when they interact, not to dictate one computational pipeline.

## 6. Position relative to existing adequacy and abstraction theories

The account is intentionally cumulative. Ecological identity theory already analyzes what makes a system the same through change (Cumming & Collier, 2005; Delettre, 2021). Ecological model-adequacy work already asks whether state variables, controls, data, and validation are sufficient for a modelling purpose (Getz et al., 2018), while general philosophy of modelling explicitly evaluates models and data for adequacy to purpose (Parker, 2020; Bokulich & Parker, 2021). State-and-Transition Models already connect ecological states to thresholds and intervention (Stringham et al., 2003). POMDP and adaptive-management theory already combine hidden state, observations, actions, and model uncertainty (Nicol & Chadès, 2012; Fackler & Pacifici, 2014). Causal states and causal abstraction already formalize predictive or interventional coarse graining (Shalizi & Crutchfield, 2001; Beckers & Halpern, 2019).

CREST should therefore not be judged by whether any one of these ingredients is new. Its potential contribution is narrower: an ecology-specific synthesis in which one proposed coarse state equivalence is subjected to four differently structured adequacy questions, each linked to a separate recovered theorem program and a different response to failure. Whether this exact four-contract mapping warrants a priority claim remains open; the argument does not depend on that priority. The manuscript is intended to remain a substantive synthesis even if no priority claim survives specialist review.

This positioning also explains why we resist collapsing CREST into causal abstraction or POMDP theory. Those frameworks are indispensable neighbors and can encode many of the relevant ingredients. The present philosophical argument is about how ecological scientists interpret the adequacy of a state variable when it is expected to carry several kinds of scientific work. The distinction is therefore at the level of representational commitments and failure diagnosis, not at the level of whether one formal super-framework can contain the variables.

## 7. Limits

The current framework is finite and theorem-guided. It does not infer the correct future grammar, source-target relation, mechanism family, evidence model, or report target from ecological data. It does not establish that the four audits exhaust all legitimate criteria for ecological representation. It does not establish a universal joint state, an audit order, or a single combined complexity measure. The exact theorems function here as conceptual benchmarks that make several hidden commitments visible; empirical adequacy still requires domain-specific modelling and evidence.

The argument also does not establish that every ecological state variable must literally be implemented as a formal quotient. Many ecological classifications are qualitative, approximate, historically negotiated, or operationally defined. The finite exact results are used as limiting cases that make the logic of several adequacy claims explicit. Approximate, probabilistic, continuous, or data-estimated versions require additional assumptions and may alter which distinctions are practically worth retaining.

Nor does CREST resolve the normative question of which scientific targets deserve priority. A management programme may value robustness over fine prediction, may tolerate ambiguity to reduce monitoring cost, or may intentionally preserve historical categories for institutional reasons. Contract-relativity means these commitments should be stated; it does not rank them without additional normative premises.

CREST is therefore best read at present as a **metatheoretical synthesis with formal anchors**, not as a completed general theory of ecology and not as a replacement for ecological identity theory, model adequacy, causal abstraction, adaptive management, or other theories of representational adequacy.

## 8. Conclusion

When ecologists assign different configurations the same state label, the merge does scientific work. It says, implicitly or explicitly, that some differences can be ignored. The four CREST audits ask four different questions about that permission: will the ignored difference matter under the declared future; does the inherited category still mean the same thing after structural change; do retained mechanisms support the same prediction; and has the evidence actually licensed the distinction the report claims? The answer need not be the same in all four cases.

The value of the distinction is diagnostic rather than taxonomic. Future insufficiency, semantic non-portability, mechanism non-robustness, and evidential non-resolvability can all make the same state label scientifically inadequate, but they do not call for the same correction. A more detailed state, a semantic repair, an ambiguity-explicit prediction, and a stronger evidence contract solve different problems. Keeping those problems visible prevents the convenience of one word—`state`—from hiding different scientific commitments.

**Ecological state equivalence should therefore be treated as an explicit scientific commitment rather than an invisible modelling default.** CREST offers one theorem-grounded way to ask what that commitment is supposed to preserve and to distinguish why it can fail. Its present contribution is deliberately limited: not a new metaphysics of ecosystems, not a universal abstraction algorithm, and not a completed theory of ecological adequacy, but a formalized philosophical vocabulary for making coarse ecological sameness inspectable.
